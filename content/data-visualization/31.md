Pairing a chart with a map enables each to add value to the other. The chart at the top shows the heights of major mountains, and the map shows where they are. Ranges are color-coded to correspond with the map below, allowing readers to move easily between the two. Notable world landmarks are shown to give a sense of scale, such as the Great Pyramid of Giza at the base of the blue Africa section. At the top of the scale is the flight altitude reached by Louis Gay-Lussac on his 1804 balloon flight. The design borrows from Alexander von Humboldt’s earlier data-driven profiles of the Andes, which helped launch a new era of visualizing geographic phenomena. Comparative mountain charts became a common visualization in geography publications in the decades that followed. Many of these showed crowded mountain peaks in one imagined landscape, but Weiland instead arranges them by mountain range along a measurable scale.
