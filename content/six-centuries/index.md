The Center’s collections offer a broad overview of the history of printed cartography. From early wood-block printed city views, to ornate Dutch maritime atlases, to the evolution of printed maps, to the modern digital era, our holdings encompass a near-infinite combination of maps for teaching and understanding how cartography has been entwined with the human experience and can be read as a reflection of our imagination.

The maps in the Center’s collections span six centuries, from the earliest years of the printing press to the present day. They trace the printed map from its earliest stages as the first modern atlas to the colorful pictorial maps of the twentieth century. These maps span the full range of cartographic media, such as manuscript, woodblock, engraving and lithography, each with its own persuasive styles and symbologies.

Instruction is central to the Center’s mission, and engaging students with the collection is one of our biggest priorities. The maps that follow invite the questions we explore together in the classroom: How was this map made? Where did its information come from? Who used it, and why? How does it represent land and people? Asking these questions also means seeing maps as products of their time, reflecting the assumptions and biases of their makers alongside their artistry and ingenuity.
