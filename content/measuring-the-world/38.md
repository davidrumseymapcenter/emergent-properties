From early on, cartographers grappled with the problem of depicting the three-dimensional earth on a two-dimensional surface. As European exploration expanded their knowledge of the world, mapmakers experimented with ways of fitting the globe on a single sheet of paper so that newly encountered lands could be seen in context. World maps showing the earth as two hemispheres were common in this era, but omitted or distorted regions near their edges. Angelocrator’s solution in this map was to split the sphere into eight sections, like slicing an apple in half and in half again to get eight equal parts, instead of one or two, reducing the apparent “stretching” of landforms. Shown here are the eight sections arranged together to depict the whole earth. Angelocrator also created another arrangement depicting the earth from its poles as two maps of the northern and southern hemispheres. Each of the eight sections take the form of what we now call a Reuleaux triangle, a curved shape of constant width that Leonardo da Vinci used a century earlier for his own experimental world map.
