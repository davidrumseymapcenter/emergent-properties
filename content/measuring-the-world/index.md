Maps and the people who made them have shaped how we understand the world, and that influence can be especially appreciated through the evolution of the Earth-measurement sciences and technology behind them. This collection looks at the history of geomatics: the science and technology behind the collecting, analyzing, and presenting spatial information. The term is modern, but it has a history starting in the analog era, represented by items such as compasses, surveying instruments, field notebooks, and map makers’ tools.

Before satellites and geographic information systems (GIS), every coastline was traced by a navigator positioning themself from celestial objects, every mountain measured by surveyors’ angles, and every accurate map built from the thousands of collective observations made by hand, recorded in notebooks, and passed on to new generations of mapmakers.

Where other collections at the Center consider maps as finished products, this one looks under the hood. Its objects, projections, declination charts, ship’s logs, field notebooks, compasses, and other sighting instruments are the working tools of measurement. They represent the analog versions of aerial photography, satellite navigation, and GIS. They let us understand the history of our evolving ability to measure the Earth, and to appreciate how much labor and ingenuity is underneath the maps in our collections.
