The rapidly industrializing 19th-century world ushered in a movement toward standardization across many fields. Guides such as *A Hand book for plain and ornamental mapping, and engineering drawing* helped establish shared cartographic conventions. Wilme, a British civil engineer and surveyor, compiled a utilitarian reference with a distinctly educational purpose: to use dozens of plates and fold-outs to show how a well-designed engraved map should be made. Its pages offer advice on lettering styles; methods for representing trees, sand, water, and mountains; and instructions for using essential drawing tools such as stencils. It was meant to both teach aspiring mapmakers, architects, and engineers, and to serve those already professionally involved in efforts by the Ordnance Survey, Britain’s national mapping agency, and the booming railway industry. This plate gathers “some of the principal characters used in mapping” into a single imagined landscape, such as hilly landscapes, fortifications, gardens, and land use––some of which are evident in maps on display here.
